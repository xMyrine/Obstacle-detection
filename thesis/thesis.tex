\begin{abstract-english}
This thesis investigates Out-of-Distribution detection using the self-supervised representations of the DINOv3 Vision Transformer. Unlike traditional global classification, our approach leverages localized semantic features by clustering image patches into a Bag of Visual Words representation. We evaluate post-hoc scoring methods, including Energy-based scores, and K-Nearest neighbours  with Chi-Square metrics, to distinguish known categories from unknown inputs. Benchmarking on datasets such as Caltech-101, Food-101, and CIFAR100 demonstrates that DINOv3’s high-dimensional latent space significantly enhances feature separation. The results show that localized hierarchical analysis improves the robustness of OOD detection, particularly for semantically ambiguous inputs.
\end{abstract-english}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{intro}
Deep learning architectures are currently experiencing a substantial surge in prevalence. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) have demonstrated remarkable performance in various computer vision tasks. However, the real world is full of unknowns, which poses serious challenges for these models. When deployed in dynamic environments, they often encounter inputs that differ significantly from their training data, leading to unreliable predictions, \textbf{as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:yolo_fail}.} The task of identifying such inputs, known as out-of-distribution (OOD) detection, is crucial for ensuring the safety and reliability of Artifical Intelligence (AI) systems.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/yolo_sign_failure.jpg}
    \caption[YOLO detection limitations on traffic signs]{Limitations of a standard You Only Look Once (YOLO) detector. The model fails to recognize the nearest `No Parking' sign (False Negative) while misclassifying distant ones as `Stop Signs'. This illustrates the necessity for robust feature representations.}
    \label{fig:yolo_fail}
\end{figure}

\vfill

Previous research has primarily focused on binary OOD detection, where models must distinguish only between in-distribution (ID) and OOD samples.\cite{wallin2025prohocprobabilistichierarchicaloutofdistribution}
While this is an important step towards robust AI, it does not fully capture the complexity of real-world scenarios. For example, when an autonomous vehicle encounters an unseen input, it is insufficient to simply flag it as OOD. Instead, a hierarchical approach assigns it to a generic class (e.g., `obstacle'), preserving semantic information essential for decision-making.

To address these challenges, this thesis explores the capabilities of self-supervised Vision Transformers, specifically the DINOv3 architecture\cite{siméoni2025dinov3}. We selected this model for its state-of-the-art performance on out-of-distribution benchmarks (as shown in ObjectNet evaluations) and its ability to produce rich semantic representations while being kept frozen. This "frozen" capability allows for substantial computational savings, making the model ideal for efficient \textbf{post-hoc} analysis without the need for extensive fine-tuning.

\section{Goals}
\label{sec:goals}

The primary objective of this thesis is to analyze DINOv3 internal representations and evaluate their suitability for classification. The specific goals are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Feature Space Visualization:} To implement a feature extraction pipeline and analyze class separability using PCA and t-SNE dimensionality reduction on datasets such as CIFAR and Oxford-IIIT Pet.
    
    \item \textbf{Attention Mechanism Analysis:} To visualize self-attention maps and interpret how the model spatially isolates semantic objects from the background without supervision.
    
    \item \textbf{Layer-wise Evolution:} To track the trajectory of [CLS] tokens across network layers, identifying the specific depth where the model begins to separate distinct semantic concepts.

    \item \textbf{Semantic Patch Clustering:} To construct a localized visual dictionary by extracting dense DINOv3 patch tokens and applying unsupervised K-Means clustering to map recurring structural patterns.
    
    \item \textbf{Dual-Condition Anomaly Detection:} To develop an OOD detection logic that flags unseen distributions by measuring both the spatial distance to known training features and the semantic chaos within the K-NN neighbourhood.
    
    \item \textbf{Comparative Performance Evaluation:} To benchmark the custom histogram-based pipeline against standard linear probing baselines (MSP, Energy, Predictive Entropy) to determine if structural feature distributions offer superior OOD robustness compared to traditional logit-based confidence scores.
\end{enumerate}


\chapter{Related Work}
\label{related_work}
This chapter reviews the existing literature relevant to the thesis. It covers the evolution of visual backbone architectures focused on Vision Transformers and Self-Supervised Learning. 
In due course, it delves into established methodologies for OOD detection. 
Finally, it explores the emerging concept of hierarchical OOD detection, highlighting its significance and the challenges it presents.

\section{Visual Representation Learning}
\label{sec:rel_representation}
State-of-the-art computer vision architectures have evolved significantly from traditional CNNs to more sophisticated models like ViTs.

\subsection{Vision Transformers}
\label{sec:rel_vit}
Vision Transformers (ViTs)\cite{dosovitskiy2021imageworth16x16words} have revolutionized and dethroned CNNs as the progressively increasing architecture in computer vision. 
Unlike CNNs, which process images via local receptive fields and convolutional layers, ViTs leverage the self-attention mechanism from the Transformer architecture\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed} to capture global dependencies in images.

\subsection{Self-Supervised Learning and DINO}
\label{sec:rel_ssl_dino}
Training ViTs typically requires massive labeled datasets. To mitigate this, Self-Supervised Learning (SSL) has emerged as a powerful alternative. Methods like DINO (Distillation with No Labels)\cite{caron2021emergingpropertiesselfsupervisedvision} and its successors, DINOv2 and DINOv3\cite{siméoni2025dinov3}\cite{oquab2024dinov2learningrobustvisual}, learn robust features by enforcing consistency between different views of the same object.

\section{Out-of-Distribution Detection}
\label{sec:rel_ood}
The aim of OOD detection is to safely distinguish between data that the model has seen during training (ID) and data that is novel or anomalous (OOD).

\subsection{Post-Hoc OOD Methods}
\label{sec:rel_posthoc}
Post-hoc OOD detection methods derive anomaly scores directly from a pre-trained model without the need for parameter modification or retraining. This property makes them computationally efficient and easily deployable in real-world scenarios.

\textbf{Maximum Softmax Probability (MSP):} The fundamental approach proposed by Hendrycks and Gimpel\cite{hendrycks2018baselinedetectingmisclassifiedoutofdistribution} assumes that a classifier's softmax output can be used to gauge uncertainty. Inputs with low maximum softmax probabilities are flagged as OOD, indicating that the model is less confident in its predictions.

\textbf{Distance based Methods:} 
To mitigate overconfidence, these methods assess the distance of an input's representation from known class distributions.
Lee at al.\cite{lee2018simpleunifiedframeworkdetecting} proposed using the \textbf{Mahalanobis Distance}, modeling the pre-softmax features of each class as a multivariate Gaussian distribution. An input is flagged as OOD if it lies far from the nearest class centroid in this latent space.

\textbf{Energy-Based Models:} Liu et al.\cite{liu2021energybasedoutofdistributiondetection} introduced an energy-based scoring function derived from the model's logits. The energy score provides a more calibrated measure of uncertainty, with lower energy values indicating higher confidence in the input being ID.

\textbf{ODIN (Out-of-Distribution detector for Neural networks):} Liang et al.\cite{liang2020enhancingreliabilityoutofdistributionimage} introduced ODIN, which improves upon the MSP baseline by combining two strategies: \textit{temperature scaling} and \textit{input perturbation}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item First, a large temperature scalar is applied to the softmax function to separate the probability distributions of ID and OOD samples.
    \item Second, small perturbations are added to the input image in the direction of the gradient to aggressively maximize the predicted softmax score for ID samples.
\end{itemize}
While effective, the requirement for input preprocessing increases computational cost during inference compared to pure output-based methods.

\subsection{Training Based OOD Methods}
\label{sec:rel_trainingbased}
While post-hoc methods operate on fixed models, training-based methods explicitly optimize the model parameters to enhance the separability between ID and OOD data during the learning phase.

\textbf{Ensemble Methods:} Deep Ensembles\cite{lakshminarayanan2017simplescalablepredictiveuncertainty} utilizes multiple models trained independently to capture uncertainty. The variance in predictions across the ensemble serves as an indicator of OOD inputs, with higher variance suggesting greater uncertainty. 
While this approach significantly improves uncertainty estimation and OOD detection accuracy, it comes with a high computational cost (both for training and inference) that is often prohibitive for real-time applications.

\textbf{Regularized Training:} Other methods introduce specific loss functions or \textit{proxy} OOD data during training. For instance, CSI \cite{king2025contextuallearninganomalydetection} utilizes contrastive learning with strong data augmentations to simulate distributional shifts. Similarly, Adversarial Reciprocal Points Learning (ARPL) \cite{repec:hin:jnlmpe:8268249,} generates \textit{reciprocal points} in the latent space to represent the unknown class. Although ARPL achieves state-of-the-art performance on many benchmarks, it requires a complex training scheme and extensive hyper-parameter tuning \cite{10350539}.

\textbf{Outlier Exposure (OE):} A direct approach involves training the model on both ID data and a large auxiliary dataset of known outliers (e.g., 80 Million Tiny Images). The model is penalized if it predicts high confidence for these outliers \cite{hendrycks2019deepanomalydetectionoutlier}. While effective, this method relies on the availability of a curated OOD dataset and may not generalize well to anomalies that are distributionally distinct from the auxiliary data.

\section{Hierarchical and Open-Set Recognition}
\label{sec:rel_hierarchical}
Standard OOD treats all unknown inputs as a single homogeneous class. Nevertheless, in many real-world applications it is beneficial
to understand semantic nature of unrecognized objects.

\subsection{From Open-Set to Open-World}
\label{sec:rel_openworld}
\textbf{Open-Set Recognition (OSR)} extends long-established classification by requiring models to accurately classify known classes while rejecting unknown classes during inference\cite{6365193}. 

\textbf{Open-World Recognition (OWR)} inclines to more a realistic approach, where the system must identify novel inputs and eventually incorporate them into its knowledge base through continuous learning\cite{Bendale_2015}.

\subsection{Hierarchical Recognition}
\label{sec:rel_hierarchical_recognition}

In many real-world applications, visual concepts naturally exhibit a taxonomic organization. For instance, in biological classification, individual species are grouped into genera, families, and orders. Hierarchical recognition leverages this inherent structure to provide predictions that are semantically meaningful even when fine-grained identification fails.

Standard classification models typically employ a flat label space, treating all misclassifications with equal severity. In this setting, confusing a \textit{dog} with a \textit{wolf} incurs the same penalty as confusing a \textit{dog} with a \textit{car}, despite the vast semantic discrepancy between these errors. This limitation was notably addressed by Bertinetto et al. \cite{bertinetto2020makingbettermistakesleveraging}, who argued that models should aim for "better mistakes" by minimizing hierarchical distance rather than just 0/1 loss.

Hierarchical models utilize structured label spaces, often derived from ontologies such as WordNet \cite{miller1995wordnet}, to enable \textit{coarse-to-fine} inference. As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:pets_hier} using the Oxford-IIIT Pet dataset \cite{6248092}, distinct categories (e.g., specific breeds) can be naturally aggregated into super-categories (e.g., \textit{Felines} vs. \textit{Canines}).
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figures/oxford_pet_III_hiearchy.png}
    \caption[Visual hierarchy in Oxford-IIIT Pet Dataset]{Visual demonstration of semantic hierarchy. Fine-grained categories (specific breeds) naturally cluster into broader super-classes (Felines and Canines). A hierarchical OOD detector can leverage this structure to classify an unknown dog breed as a generic `Canine' rather than rejecting it completely.}
    \label{fig:pets_hier}
\end{figure}
In the context of OOD detection, this structure provides a robust fallback mechanism. If the model encounters a novel input that implies high uncertainty at the leaf level, it can back-off to a parent node (e.g., classifying an unknown animal simply as \textit{Animal}), thereby preserving system reliability and safety.

\chapter{Feature Representation and Analysis}
\label{our_research}
The initial phase of this research consisted of assessing the efficacy of DINOv3 feature representations for our OOD problem. A critical requirement for our task is the model's ability to enforce tight grouping of semantically similar classes in the feature space while maintaining separability from distinct concepts.

To verify this property, we extracted feature embeddings from diverse datasets and applied dimensionality reduction techniques such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and t-Distributed Stochastic neighbour Embedding (t-SNE). 

\section{Dataset Selection}
\label{sec:dataset_selection}
To evaluate the robustness and generalizability of the features learned by the DINOv3 model, we employed a suite of standard computer vision benchmark datasets, alongside a custom dataset to test domain shifts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CIFAR-10} \cite{Krizhevsky2009LearningML}: A widely utilized benchmark consisting of 60,000 $32 \times 32$ colour images across 10 distinct classes, with 6,000 images per class. The standard split includes 50,000 training images and 10,000 test images.
    \item \textbf{CIFAR-100} \cite{Krizhevsky2009LearningML}: Similar in structure to CIFAR-10 but significantly more challenging due to its fine-grained nature. It contains 100 classes with 600 images each, split into 500 training and 100 testing images per class.
    \item \textbf{SVHN (Street View House Numbers)} \cite{Netzer2011ReadingDI}: A real-world image dataset designed for developing machine learning and object recognition algorithms. It comprises over 600,000 labeled digits ($0-9$) cropped from house numbers in Google Street View imagery, presenting a distinct distribution shift from standard object datasets.
    \item \textbf{Food-101} \cite{bossard2014food}: A large-scale dataset for fine-grained food recognition, consisting of 101,000 images representing 101 different food categories. Each category contains 1,000 images, offering a high variance in visual appearance within classes.
    \item \textbf{Oxford-IIIT Pet} \cite{6248092}: A dataset containing 7,349 images of 37 different breeds of cats and dogs. Each image is annotated with a breed label and a pixel-level segmentation mask, making it suitable for both classification and segmentation tasks.
    \item \textbf{Pokémon Dataset}: A custom dataset containing images of various Pokémon species. This dataset was introduced to evaluate the model's ability to generalize to unseen, synthetic classes that differ significantly from natural images.
\end{itemize}

\section{Feature representation via PCA}
\label{sec:feature_representation_pca}
Prior to investigating the layer-wise evolution of features within the DINOv3 architecture, we conducted an initial assessment of the overall quality of the learned representations. This involved extracting feature embeddings from the final layer of the model for images across the selected datasets. 

\subsection{Dimensionality Reduction and Visualization}
To visuallize the high-dimensional feature space of the data and demonstrate class separability, we employed \textbf{PCA} to reduce the dimensionality of the embeddings from $\mathbb{R}^{4096}$ to $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$.

The goal was to project the \textit{final} representations of either the entire dataset or a subset onto a common manifold. This visualization helped us to confirm the following properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intra-class Compactness:} Samples of the same class are grouped tightly together.
    \item \textbf{Inter-class Separability:} Different classes form distinct, non-overlapping clusters.
\end{itemize}

The analysis of the Pokémon dataset presents a more complex distribution, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pokemon_pca}. While some classes form distinct clusters, the boundaries are significantly not as well-defined as in the natural image datasets.

We attribute this behavior to high \textbf{intra-class domain variance}. Unlike standard datasets where each class has a relatively consistent visual appearance, the Pokémon dataset mixes heterogeneous sources:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stylistic variance:} 2D sprites, 3D renders, anime frames, and real-world photos of merchandise.
    \item \textbf{Feature mismatch:} Visually dissimilar instances can share the same label, reducing embedding compactness.
\end{itemize}

Consequently, the model likely creates multiple sub-clusters for a single class based on these artistic domains rather than merging them into a single unified entity, resulting in the observed ``sketchy'' separation.

Furthermore, background clutter often occupies more area than the digit itself. Without fine-tuning, the encoder likely prioritizes these background textures over the structural signal of the class label.

\section{Feature representation via t-SNE}
\label{sec:feature_representation_tsne}

While PCA effectively demonstrates the separability of classes, it is inherently a linear technique. To ensure that we did not overlook complex, non-linear structures in the feature space, we also applied \textbf{t-Distributed Stochastic neighbour Embedding} \cite{JMLR:v9:vandermaaten08a,}.

\subsection{Motivation and Methodology}
Unlike PCA, t-SNE is a probabilistic technique designed to preserve local neighbourhood structures. It is particularly effective at revealing clusters in data where the relationship between classes is non-linear. We hypothesized that t-SNE might uncover finer sub-structures or better resolve classes that appeared only weakly separated in the linear PCA projection (e.g., some Pokémon classes).

\subsection{Comparative Analysis: Cleaner Separation and Non-Linearity}
The application of t-SNE yielded a distinct visual improvement over PCA, producing significantly cleaner and more compact clusters. By prioritizing local neighbourhood preservation, t-SNE was able to resolve boundaries that appeared diffuse in the linear projection.

\section{Layer-wise Feature Evolution and Trajectory Analysis}
\label{sec:visualization}

To effectively analyze the internal dynamics of the DINOv3 architecture~\cite{siméoni2025dinov3}, we developed a visualization pipeline capable of mapping the high-dimensional latent space of  dimensions into a 3-dimensional RGB colour space. This process, which we refer to as a ``Layer-wise Feature Trajectory,'' allows for the observation of semantic evolution as information propagates from the input layers to the deep semantic layers. The use of PCA to inspect the semantic layout of self-supervised features follows methodologies established in DINO-based research~\cite{siméoni2025dinov3}.

\subsection{Data Extraction and Pre-processing}
The extraction mechanism relies on registering \textit{forward hooks} within the PyTorch framework. We iterate through the model's architecture to identify the transformer blocks (specifically \texttt{model.encoder.layer}) and intercept the output tensor $O^{(l)}$ at each layer $l \in \{0, \dots, L-1\}$.

For a given input image, the output at layer $l$ is a tensor of shape $(1, N_{seq}, D)$, where $N_{seq}$ represents the sequence length and $D$ is the embedding dimension. To visualize the spatial layout of features, we discard the special tokens—specifically the \texttt{[CLS]} token and the register tokens retaining only the patch tokens. These patch tokens are then reshaped back into a 2D grid corresponding to the original image topology ($H \times W$).

\subsection{Dimensionality Reduction Strategies}
Visualizing $D=4096$ dimensions requires projecting the data onto a lower-dimensional manifold. We implemented and compared two distinct strategies for this projection using PCA: \textit{Global PCA} and \textit{Local PCA}.

\subsubsection{Method A: Global PCA (Trajectory Preservation)}
\label{sec:method_a_global_pca}
In the Global approach, we prioritize the temporal and geometric consistency of the features across the network depth. We aggregate the feature vectors from all layers $l$ and all spatial positions into a single global dataset $\mathbf{X}_{global}$:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{X}_{global} = \bigcup_{l=0}^{L-1} \mathbf{X}^{(l)}
\end{equation}

To ensure that feature representations from different depths of the network are geometrically comparable, we compute a single, unified transformation matrix $W_{global}$. This is achieved by fitting the PCA estimator on the aggregated dataset $\mathbf{X}_{global}$, effectively forcing the algorithm to learn a "global dictionary" of variance that encompasses the model's entire inference process—from raw pixel statistics in shallow layers to high-level semantic abstractions in deep layers.

Mathematically, this identifies the three principal components that explain the maximum variance across the \textit{full hierarchy} of the network. Once this fixed basis $W_{global}$ is established, we project the specific feature tensor $\mathbf{X}^{(l)}$ of every individual layer $l$ onto these static axes. By using a constant projection matrix for all layers, we ensure that the resulting colour shifts in the visualization represent genuine geometric transformations of the feature vectors (e.g., the data manifold rotating from a "pixel-correlation" subspace to a "semantic" subspace) rather than arbitrary statistical realignments between frames.

\textbf{Visual Outcome:} This method results in a smooth colour progression. We observed a distinct shift from red hues in initial layers to azure/cyan hues in deeper layers. This indicates that the feature subspace occupied by raw visual patterns (shallow layers) is mathematically orthogonal to the subspace of semantic abstractions (deep layers). The colour shift effectively visualizes the ``drift'' of the image representation through the high-dimensional space. \textbf{This trajectory is visually summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:global_pca_grid}, where the subject (`Cat') transitions from a pixel-dominated red representation to a semantically abstract azure representation. This semantic shift occurs as deeper layers aggregate local patches across expanded receptive fields, discarding low-level pixel variance to capture global, context-invariant object concepts.}

\subsubsection{Method A.1: Global PCA with Mean Subtraction (Centered PCA)}

In standard PCA, it is customary to center the data by subtracting the empirical mean before calculating the covariance matrix. To investigate the effect of this centering on our feature trajectory visualization, we conducted an auxiliary experiment where the global dataset $\mathbf{X}_{global}$ was mean-centered prior to projection:

\begin{equation}
    \bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{x}_{i}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{X}}_{global} = \mathbf{X}_{global} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}
\end{equation}

where $N$ is the total number of feature vectors across all layers $l$ and spatial positions. The PCA estimator was subsequently fitted on the centered dataset $\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{global}$.

\textbf{Visual Outcome and Analysis:} Contrary to the uncentered Global PCA, applying mean subtraction resulted in a severe degradation of visual clarity. The resulting projections produced dim, desaturated, ``mud-like'' representations with arbitrary blue hues.

This phenomenon can be explained by the geometric behavior of feature maps in deep neural networks. As features are processed from shallow to deep layers, they exhibit a massive directional shift through the high-dimensional space. In the uncentered approach \ref{sec:method_a_global_pca}, the primary principal components naturally align with this dominant drift trajectory, mapping the large magnitudes to bright, distinct RGB values. 

Conversely, when the global mean is subtracted, this primary trajectory vector collapses to the origin. The centered PCA is forced to capture only the localized variance surrounding the global centroid. Because the magnitude of this localized variance is relatively small compared to the scale of the layer-wise drift, the projected values lack the amplitude required to drive the RGB channels to bright values. The overlapping, low-magnitude variances consequently manifest as dark, ambiguous colours, with specific hues (such as blue) dominating only if a particular variance direction aligns heavily with that colour channel.

\subsubsection{Method B: Local PCA (Contrast Maximization)}
In the Local approach, we prioritize the maximum statistical separation within a specific layer, disregarding the global context. For each individual layer $l$, we instantiate a new PCA estimator and fit it solely on the features $\mathbf{X}^{(l)}$ of that specific layer.

\begin{equation}
    V^{(l)}_{local} = \text{PCA}_{l}(\mathbf{X}^{(l)})
\end{equation}

The resulting projection is normalized independently for each frame.

\textbf{Visual Outcome and Utility:}
While this method sacrifices the ability to track the global trajectory, it offers superior \textit{local discriminability}. 

In the Global view, the dominant variance is the \textbf{depth} signal (Input vs. Output), which can overpower subtle spatial details. By re-fitting the PCA locally, we filter out this depth signal and force the visualization to answer a specific question: \textit{``What are the most significant differences between patches at this specific stage of processing?''}

This renders fine-grained details visible that are otherwise washed out in the Global view:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Shallow Layers:} It reveals high-frequency texture boundaries and edge orientations that the model is attending to.
    \item \textbf{Deep Layers:} It highlights subtle semantic separations (e.g., distinguishing between a dog's ear and its head) which might represent only a fraction of the total global variance but are critical for the final classification task.
\end{itemize}

Therefore, Local PCA acts as a high-gain contrast filter, useful for inspecting the granular attention mechanisms of specific blocks without the bias of the network's overall depth trajectory.

\textbf{Visual Outcome:} This results in visualizations with maximum local contrast, highlighting the most salient features such as edges and textures at every specific depth. However, this method sacrifices temporal consistency. Because the eigenvectors are re-calculated at every step, the direction of the principal components can flip arbitrarily (the \textit{sign ambiguity} problem in PCA). This manifests as abrupt, non-semantic colour inversions between consecutive layers, making it difficult to track the evolution of a specific concept. \textbf{The effect of this local contrast maximization is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:local_pca_grid}. While fine-grained details are visible, the colours hold no global meaning, changing primarily due to statistical variations within the layer rather than semantic evolution.}

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}
The comparison between these two approaches highlights a trade-off between \textit{local discriminability} and \textit{global coherence}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Global PCA} is superior for analyzing the network's hierarchical structure. It serves as a visual proof that DinoV3 processes information sequentially, moving the data manifold from a ``pixel-correlation'' space to a ``semantic-correlation'' space~\cite{siméoni2025dinov3}.
    \item \textbf{Local PCA} is superior for inspecting the granular attention of specific layers. It reveals that even in intermediate layers, the model separates foreground and background with high variance, although this separation is not yet aligned with the final semantic output.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Spatial Attention Dynamics: The Scanning Protocol}
Having analyzed the hierarchical evolution of features across the network depth (chapter~\ref{sec:visualization}). We now turn our focus to the spatial relationships encoded within the final semantic representations. Specifically, we aim to interpret the \textit{self-attention mechanism} of the DINOv3 architecture to understand how different image regions semantically \textbf{connect} to one another.

\subsection{Methodology: Attention Matrix Extraction}
The core idea is to extract raw attention weights from the final Transformer block of our model. For a given input image, we forward pass it and intercept the 
self-attention weights $A$ from the last layer. The raw attention tensor has the shape $(H, N, N)$ where $H$ is the number of heads and $N$ is the sequence length (tokens).

To obtain a unified spatial map, we average the weights across all attention heads and discard the special \texttt{[CLS]} and register tokens, resulting in a patch-to-patch attention matrix $A_{patch} \in \mathbb{R}^{P \times P}$, where $P$ is the number of spatial patches.

\subsection{The Scanning Mechanism}
The visualization is constructed as a temporal sequence where each frame represents a specific spatial query:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Query Selection (The Green Box):} We define a sliding window of size $k \times k$ patches that traverses the image grid in a raster scan pattern. This window represents the region of interest $Q_{pos}$.
    \item \textbf{Attention Integration:} For each window position, we aggregate the attention weights originating from the patches inside the window. This computes a heatmap $M$ representing the cumulative attention of the query region:
    \begin{equation}
        M = \sum_{i \in Q_{pos}} A_{patch}[i, :]
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Visual Overlay:} The resulting heatmap $M$ is normalized and overlaid onto the original image.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Visual Interpretation}
The results provide a granular view of the model's internal object segmentation. As the green query box moves over an object, the attention heatmap instantly ``lights up'' the entire semantic entity, demonstrating that the local patches are attending globally to the object they belong to.

When the query focuses on one eye, the attention map strongly activates the contralateral eye, linking them as a single semantic concept. Similarly, querying the nose region triggers attention across the whiskers, suggesting the model has learned to group these fine-grained features together.

\subsection{Linear Probe and Scoring Functions}
Foundation models such as DINOv3 inherently output high-dimensional feature embeddings rather than class-specific logits. However, several prominent post-hoc OOD detection algorithms operate directly on the logit space of a classifier. To bridge this gap without distorting the pre-trained manifold via full fine-tuning, we trained a simple Linear Probe on top of the frozen DINOv3 embeddings. This yielded the necessary pre-softmax logits for our target ID classes (Food-101).

Using these projections, we evaluated three state-of-the-art OOD scoring mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Energy Score \cite{liu2021energybasedoutofdistributiondetection}:} Computes the Helmholtz free energy over the logit vector, functioning as a theoretically grounded alternative to the standard softmax confidence.
    \item \textbf{Mahalanobis Distance \cite{lee2018simpleunifiedframeworkdetecting}:} A generative, distance-based metric that measures the distance of a test sample's feature representation to the closest class-conditional Gaussian distribution fitted on the ID training features.
    \item \textbf{ODIN (Out-of-Distribution detector for Neural networks) \cite{liang2020enhancingreliabilityoutofdistributionimage}:} Utilizes temperature scaling and input preprocessing (small adversarial perturbations) to separate the softmax distributions of ID and OOD samples.
\end{itemize}

The primary evaluation metric used was the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC). Additionally, for qualitative analysis, we set the operating acceptance threshold to maintain a 95\% True Positive Rate (TPR) for ID data.

\subsection{Results and the Semantic Overlap Anomaly}
The quantitative results, visualized in the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves in Figure~\ref{fig:roc_curves}, demonstrated near-flawless OOD detection performance (AUROC $\approx 1.0$) when comparing Food-101 against the Oxford-IIIT Pet and FGVC Aircraft datasets across all three methods. The steep, right-angled trajectories for these datasets confirm that the representations learned by the network cleanly separated these distinct semantic domains.

However, as evident from the visibly sagging curves in Figure~\ref{fig:roc_curves}, performance on the Textures dataset presented a notable exception, yielding lower AUROC scores and higher False Positive Rates. Upon qualitative investigation, we discovered that this drop in statistical performance does not indicate a failure of the feature representation. Rather, it highlights a semantic overlap between the datasets: the Textures dataset inherently contains images of distinct food patterns (e.g., clustered berries, cross-sections of vegetables, woven pasta). 

Consequently, the models confidently and correctly, from a semantic standpoint classified these specific OOD samples as ID (Food). To investigate this behavior, we extracted the highest-confidence false positive samples for both the Energy Score and Mahalanobis Distance at the 95\% TPR threshold, presented in Figure~\ref{fig:ood_false_positives}. It is visually evident from these visual samples that the ``misclassified'' texture images are, in fact, food items. 

\textit{Note on ODIN Evaluation:} Due to the heavy computational overhead introduced by the iterative gradient-based input perturbations required for ODIN, we evaluated this specific method on a randomly sampled subset of the data. While the resulting ROC curve for ODIN (Figure~\ref{fig:roc_curves}c) displays a perfect 100\% AUROC on this specific subset, applying it to the overlapping food-texture samples yields the same semantically justified ``failures'' observed in the Energy and Mahalanobis methods.

\section{K-Means Clustering of Patch Representations}
\label{sec:kmeans_clustering}

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Experimental Setup and Feature Extraction}
\label{sec:kmeans_setup}
% ---------------------------------------------------------
In order to investigate the localized semantic representations learned by the DINOv3 model, an unsupervised clustering approach was applied to individual image patches. High-resolution images of various mammals were selected to determine whether the model captures structural and semantic consistencies across different species within the same biological class. To ensure uniform processing, all input images were scaled to a standardized resolution of $512 \times 512$ pixels.

The DINOv3 processes each image by subdividing it into non-overlapping patches of size $16 \times 16$ pixels. For each patch, a high-dimensional feature vector was extracted from the deeper layers of the model. 

\subsection{Clustering Methodology}
\label{sec:kmeans_methodology}
% ---------------------------------------------------------
To group the dense feature vectors into distinct visual concepts, the K-Means clustering algorithm was applied. The number of clusters, denoted as $K$, was evaluated over the range $K \in [1,16]$ using the Elbow method \cite{Kodinariya2013ReviewOD}. Since the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) curve did not exhibit a pronounced elbow, $K=6$ was selected empirically as it provided a suitable balance between semantic separation and cluster granularity for the downstream classification pipeline.

    \label{fig:elbow_method}
\end{figure}

Following the clustering phase, a discrete visual representation was constructed by mapping each patch to a specific colour corresponding to its assigned cluster centroid. A comprehensive composite figure (Figure~\ref{fig:kmeans_composite}) was generated to contrast the original images directly with their corresponding cluster-coloured patch maps, allowing for an evaluation of the feature distributions.

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Spatial Shifts and Positional Encodings}
\label{sec:kmeans_spatial}
% ---------------------------------------------------------
A notable phenomenon observed in the resulting visual maps is a discernible spatial shift in the cluster assignments, where patches appear slightly offset to the left or right relative to the structural boundaries in the original image. Furthermore, the clustering algorithm explicitly differentiates between the background space on the left side of the subject and the background on the right. 

This behavior is directly attributable to the positional encodings inherent to the Vision Transformer architecture. Because the standard self-attention mechanism is inherently permutation-invariant, Transformers inject spatial awareness by adding positional embeddings to the patch tokens early in the network. These embeddings are frequently constructed using harmonic functions---specifically intersecting sine and cosine waves of varying frequencies---or learned spatial biases. Consequently, the extracted feature tokens heavily retain this spatial metadata. The K-Means algorithm, computing distances in this combined feature space, therefore partitions the patches not only by their semantic visual content but also by their absolute spatial coordinates within the frame.

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Semantic Groupings and Terrain Analysis}
\label{sec:kmeans_semantic}
% ---------------------------------------------------------
Beyond spatial partitioning, the cluster maps reveal highly specialized semantic groupings. The foreground animal shapes are clearly segmented from the background, and the background itself is further divided into vertical boundaries and the underlying floor or terrain. 

An interesting anomaly was observed in images containing zebras, which predominantly formed an isolated cluster. As highlighted in the composite visualization (Figure~\ref{fig:kmeans_composite}), we hypothesize that this divergence occurs because the zebra images feature a herd or multiple intersecting animals, creating a pattern that the model maps differently compared to the single-subject compositions of other mammals.

Additionally, the representations demonstrate a strong dependency on the underlying terrain upon which the subjects are standing. The clustering algorithm successfully decoupled different floor textures, consistently assigning grassy terrains to a specific cluster, denoted by a pink hue in the visualization, while non-grassy or barren surfaces were segregated into a separate cluster, represented in dark blue. This distinction is visually evident in the overall mappings (Figure~\ref{fig:kmeans_composite}), highlighting the capacity of DINOv3 to simultaneously encode intricate local textures, broad environmental contexts, and spatial priors within its patch-level representations.

\subsection{Feature Distribution and Histogram Analysis}
\label{sec:kmeans_histogram_analysis}
% ---------------------------------------------------------
Following the establishment of this localized visual dictionary, we sought to quantify the overall semantic composition of each image. Rather than treating an image as a spatial grid of patches, it was abstracted into a Bag of Visual Words (BoVW) by computing the frequency distribution of its assigned cluster labels. 

To ensure fair comparability across images that may vary slightly in their total number of extracted patches due to cropping dimensions, the raw frequency histograms were subjected to $L_1$ normalization. Let $c_i$ represent the raw count of patches assigned to the $i$-th cluster. The normalized bin value, $h_i$, is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    h_i = \frac{c_i}{\sum_{j} c_j}
\end{equation}
This normalization ensures that the sum of all bins in the histogram equals $1$, effectively converting the cluster counts into a discrete probability distribution that represents the proportional semantic makeup of the image (depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:cluster_histograms}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figures/clustering/histograms_clusters_animals.png}
    \caption[$L_1$-normalized cluster histograms]{$L_1$-normalized frequency histograms of patch clusters for the analyzed subjects. Each bar represents the proportional area of the image occupied by a specific semantic concept (e.g., body, head, legs).}
    \label{fig:cluster_histograms}
\end{figure}

To mathematically assess the similarity between these diverse feature distributions, the Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) distance metric was employed. The $\chi^2$ distance is highly effective for comparing frequency histograms and is defined for two normalized histograms $x$ and $y$ as:
\begin{equation}
    \chi^2(x, y) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i} \frac{(x_i - y_i)^2}{x_i + y_i}
\end{equation}

The pairwise distances between all subjects were computed and are visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:chi_square_comparison}. A notable, albeit counterintuitive, observation derived from this matrix is the relative proximity of the car's feature distribution to those of the biological animals. While a car inherently lacks biological anatomy, its fundamental compositional structure—possessing a defined "head" (front fascia/hood), a "body" (cabin), and "legs" (wheels)—is mapped into the DINOv3 latent space in a manner that yields a remarkably similar normalized cluster distribution. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/clustering/chi_square_animals_cropped.png}
    \caption[Chi-Square distance matrix for cluster distributions]{Pairwise Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) distance matrix comparing the $L_1$-normalized cluster histograms of different subjects.}
    \label{fig:chi_square_comparison}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Distance Functions}
\label{sec:knn_classification_distance_functions}
For the K-NN classification itself, we evaluated the model across a diverse set of neighbourhood sizes, testing $k \in \{1, 3, 7, 9, 11, 25\}$. To determine the most effective method for comparing our histogram representations, we assessed three distinct distance and similarity metrics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Chi-Square Distance:} A standard and highly effective metric for comparing empirical probability distributions, which aligns with the histogram format of our BoVW approach.
    \item \textbf{TF-IDF with Cosine Similarity:} Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency weighting applied to the raw histograms, followed by cosine similarity. This adapts a proven technique from information retrieval to down-weight ubiquitous visual features and emphasize discriminative ones.
    \item \textbf{Hellinger Distance (via FAISS L2):} A robust measure for quantifying the similarity between two probability distributions, computed efficiently by applying L2 distance on square-root normalized histograms within the FAISS framework.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:knn_classification_dataset}
Our evaluation was conducted using the Caltech-101 dataset. To rigorously assess the robustness of the learned representations and the influence of background noise on the clustering space, we performed the feature extraction process under three distinct data conditions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Original Images:} The standard Caltech-101 images without any spatial modification.
    \item \textbf{Cropped Foreground Segmentation:} Images segmented to isolate the foreground object, with the background replaced by zero-value (black) pixels and zoomed in. This serves as a baseline to evaluate the impact of masking out background context.
    \item \textbf{Filtered Object Patches:} A sparse extraction approach where only the specific visual patches overlapping with the foreground object mask are retained and passed to the clustering pipeline.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Proposed Dual-Condition Out-of-Distribution Detection}
\label{chap:proposed_ood_detection}
Building upon the feature representations and clustering mechanisms explored in the previous chapters, this final analytical chapter introduces an OOD detection heuristic. We extend the K-Nearest neighbours classification pipeline established in Section \ref{sec:knn_classification_results_original}, leveraging the semantic histograms of image patches to separate ID samples from unseen anomalies.

\section{Methodology: The Dual-Condition Heuristic}
\label{sec:ood_methodology}
Our approach shifts away from traditional post-hoc continuous scoring functions and treats OOD detection as a strict rule-based anomaly rejection task within the K-NN latent space. 

To determine whether an inference sample belongs to the ID manifold, we compare its histogram to the training set using the \textbf{Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) distance metric} like in \ref{sec:knn_classification}. However, relying solely on a distance threshold is insufficient due to the variable density of the latent space. Therefore, we introduce a dual-condition verification system that must trigger simultaneously to flag an image as OOD.

\subsection{Distance Threshold ($T$) and Label Chaos ($C$)}
\label{sec:distance_threshold_and_label_chaos}

Let $x$ be an inference query and $N_K(x)$ be the set of its $K$ nearest neighbours in the ID training set. The two criteria are defined as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Distance Threshold ($T$):} We evaluate the $\chi^2$ distance to the closest neighbour. If the distance exceeds a predefined threshold $T$, the sample is considered structurally distant from the known data manifold.
    \item \textbf{neighbourhood Label Chaos ($C$) and Semantic Consistency:} We introduce $C$, the maximum allowable unique classes within the $K$ nearest neighbours, as a proxy for local semantic consistency. Within the framework of information theory, $C$ correlates with the Shannon entropy of the neighbourhood's class distribution:
    
    $$H(x) = - \sum_{i=1}^{C} p_i \log(p_i)$$
    
    where $p_i$ is the fraction of neighbours in $N_K(x)$ belonging to class $i$. ID queries typically form dense, homogeneous clusters ($H(x) \approx 0$), whereas OOD samples map to sparse or overlapping regions, yielding mixed, high-entropy neighbourhoods. By thresholding $C$, we filter these high-entropy OOD inputs, restricting accepted predictions to stable, consistent local manifolds.
\end{enumerate}

To rigorously define the OOD detection boundary, we first formalize the local neighbourhood of a query sample $x$. Let $N_K(x)$ denote the set of the $K$ nearest neighbours to $x$ in the representation space, defined as:
\begin{equation}
    N_K(x) = \{(x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \dots, (x_K, y_K)\}
\end{equation}
where $x_i$ represents the feature vector of the $i$-th neighbour and $y_i \in \mathcal{Y}$ is its corresponding ground-truth label.

To quantify the local semantic consistency, we isolate the set of labels present within this neighbourhood:
\begin{equation}
    Y_K(x) = \{y_i \mid (x_i, y_i) \in N_K(x)\}
\end{equation}
Because $Y_K(x)$ is a set, it inherently contains only unique elements. Consequently, the cardinality of this set, $|Y_K(x)|$, yields the exact number of unique classes within the $K$-nearest neighbours.

The final decision boundary dictates that a sample is flagged as OOD if and only if both the $\chi^2$ distance exceeds a threshold $T$ and the neighbourhood label cardinality strictly exceeds the chaos limit $C$. We express this via the indicator function $\mathbb{I}_{\text{OOD}}(x)$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{I}_{\text{OOD}}(x) = 
    \begin{cases} 
      1 & \text{if } D_{\chi^2}(x, N_K(x)) > T \land |Y_K(x)| > C \\
      0 & \text{otherwise}
   \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Limitations: The Low-Resolution Constraint}
\label{sec:ood_limitations_resolution}

While the proposed Dual-Condition heuristic demonstrates state-of-the-art performance on high-resolution datasets, its efficacy degrades catastrophically when applied to low-resolution imagery. To quantify this limitation, we replicated our baseline experiments using \textbf{CIFAR-100} ($32 \times 32$ pixels) as the ID dataset. 

The structural failure of our method in this domain is directly tied to the architectural constraints of the DINOv3 backbone. DINOv3 processes images by extracting fixed $16 \times 16$ pixel patches. Consequently, a $32 \times 32$ CIFAR-100 image yields a grid of exactly $2 \times 2$ (only 4 patches). 

Attempting to build a robust BoVW semantic histogram from merely 4 tokens is mathematically futile. The resulting feature distributions are too sparse for the K-Means clustering and K-NN classification pipeline to extract meaningful structural variance. In contrast, ViTs specifically trained on lower resolutions with drastically smaller patch sizes (e.g., $4 \times 4$) would be required to yield a sufficiently dense patch distribution for our method to operate.

As illustrated in Table \ref{tab:ood_cifar_failure}, the extreme sparsity causes our proposed method to collapse completely. While baseline methods like Energy-Based scoring and Deep Ensembles degrade gracefully on CIFAR-100, our spatial patch-based approach plummets to an average AUROC of 72.81\% and a practically unusable FPR95 of 69.52\%.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{conclusion}

This thesis comprehensively investigated the viability of utilizing the DINOv3 Vision Transformer as a foundational architecture for hierarchical image classification and OOD detection. By systematically analyzing the model's internal representations without task-specific fine-tuning, we evaluated its capacity to inherently encode semantic hierarchies and spatially isolate visual concepts. Our methodology—spanning dimensionality reduction, self-attention scanning, and unsupervised patch clustering—demonstrated that DINOv3 establishes a highly discriminative, object-centric latent space. We successfully transitioned from analyzing raw, high-dimensional patch embeddings to constructing an interpretable semantic dictionary through BoVW and K-NN classification, validating the network's ability to abstract raw pixel statistics into unified semantic entities.

\section{Computational and Theoretical Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

While the DINOv3 architecture provides highly discriminative semantic representations, this performance comes at a substantial computational and spatial cost. The extraction of dense, patch-level embeddings imposes significant memory constraints and inference latency, primarily driven by the $O(N^2)$ algorithmic complexity of the self-attention mechanism with respect to sequence length. Consequently, processing high-resolution images or scaling BoVW codebook size introduces a severe computational bottleneck. 

Furthermore, the deployment of a K-Nearest neighbours classifier over a high-dimensional continuous latent space requires extensive memory allocation to store the reference support set, limiting its viability for real-time or edge-device deployment. From a statistical learning perspective, the immense parameter count and theoretical capacity of the Vision Transformer—reflecting an exceptionally high Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension—pose inherent risks of overfitting if adapted naively. Although our methodology mitigates this by utilizing the model as a strictly frozen, post-hoc feature extractor, the underlying structural complexity remains a fundamental trade-off between semantic abstraction capabilities and algorithmic efficiency.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
In terms of academic and practical contributions, this research provides a rigorous empirical framework for understanding the internal dynamics of frozen foundation models in the context of anomaly detection. We effectively bridged the gap between raw feature extraction and interpretable classification mapping. Furthermore, we empirically exposed the model's inherent texture bias, most notably through its failure to linearly separate geometric datasets like SVHN—and formally documented the semantic overlap anomaly, where traditional distance-based scoring functions falter due to shared underlying visual components rather than feature degradation. By comprehensively mapping both the capabilities and the structural limitations of self-supervised representations, this thesis offers researchers and practitioners critical insights necessary for the safe and predictable deployment of Vision Transformers in open-world environments.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}
Building upon these findings, there is a compelling avenue for future research utilizing the proposed clustering algorithm to establish robust hierarchical OOD detection frameworks. Our experiments indicate a strong, inherent morphological affinity within the constructed visual codebooks. For instance, we observed that broader taxonomic categories, such as mammals and other animals, consistently shared a significant proportion of semantic clusters. Similarly, structurally analogous inanimate items, such as sharp objects (e.g., scissors and swords), exhibited a remarkably high feature overlap. These natural cluster intersections strongly suggest that when the model encounters an unseen or OOD sample, it projects the unknown input into a structurally related subspace rather than generating arbitrary noise. Future work should focus on mathematically leveraging this shared semantic vocabulary to develop a dynamic, coarse-to-fine hierarchical classifier—one that gracefully backs-off to predicting a generic super-category (e.g., ``animal'' or ``sharp tool'') when fine-grained leaf-level identification fails, thereby drastically improving the reliability and interpretability of modern AI systems.
